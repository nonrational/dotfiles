# Prose Register

Do not announce the skill. Produce the prose.

## The rule the rest hangs on

**A hard line needs a breath next to it.** Vary the pressure, not only the length. Three
declaratives in a row are a drumbeat; the fourth one lands only if the third let up.

When a paragraph is all jab, the reader stops feeling any of it.

## The baseline

- No em-dashes. Full stops, semicolons, parentheses, en-dashes.
- Concrete nouns over abstractions. Strong verbs over adverbs.
- Short sentences as impact. They are punctuation, never the baseline.
- No throat-clearing, no "In today's world", no "It's important to note".
- No therapy voice. No meta ("in this essay", "as a writer").
- Open with substance. Open on the state of play, not on another writer's take; a cited author is a reference in the first paragraph, never the hook.
- Close without summary, and stop where the image lands. A clause tacked after the landing (*on each side of the table, and I don't think our way of working has caught up*) blunts it.
- Lead with the verdict. When a paragraph judges something (a reply, a review, a habit), the judgment opens it and the reasons follow. One that walks the reader through the mechanics and arrives at the judgment last is thinking out loud. A paragraph that assembles a case and lands on its closing line (*Brightness, recognition of good work, and humor help build capital to be spent on critique.*) is a different shape, and it stays.

## Moves other style guides ban

**"Not X, but Y" is allowed.** It is the signature move. It earns its place when the
reversal *is* the argument, not when it is a pivot for rhythm.

> not unreviewed code, but un-authored knowledge, severed from any knower

> The unease was never that the work is bad. It is that the work no longer implies a worker.

**Questions may warm, not only cut.**

> How do you make sociology approachable to an engineer? Pretend it's math.

> How often is a review suggestion flat wrong? Rarely.

**Three-part lists are fine when the last item does work the first two don't.**

> throwaway games, little toys, a script I will run once and never think about again

The third item is rueful and specific; the first two are setup. Contrast a failure:
"remix, retool, reimagine". Alliterative, decorative, three ways of saying one thing.

**Connectives are joints, not filler.** "Which raises the question:", "Therefore,", "Yes,",
"But if I ask you to…", "So", "I mean,". They give the reader somewhere to breathe. Cut the scene-setting;
keep the joints. A joint reused across paragraphs (three in a row that open on *So*) is cadence, not a drumbeat.

**Hedges of precision stay. Hedges of cowardice go.**

- Keep: "roughly $165,000", "usually sufficient", "mostly it looks like revision", "sufficiently good" (good enough, not excellent)
- Cut: "because I believe that", "a decent amount of time", "I think maybe"

## Prohibitions

- **No flex.** Never a sentence that flatters the writer. If a line's real work is showing how generous or sharp you are, cut it. Thank people rather than reporting that you did: *(Thanks for the idea!)*, not *I let them know, and thanked them for the idea!* A plain report of effort that sets up the next sentence (*So I've worked hard to coax agents into sounding more like me out of the gate*) is not a flex; its work is the setup.
- **Never claim the reader's reaction.** Make the case and stop. No "this is why you can trust me". Trust is the reader's to award. Naming what you are trying not to do to them (*without my readers feeling like I've handed them something cheap*) is your stake, not their verdict.
- **No grandiosity.** Don't grant a machine a stake it cannot have.
- **Don't shrink the work.** A light line that invents a deadline or a scene sells the work as faster, easier or more dramatic than it is: *have your own by lunch*, *proves itself by Tuesday*, *a stranger's shell script by flashlight*. Rueful specificity is true; these are made up. If the work still needs babysitting, the prose says so.
- **Nothing floral.** "The people who lit my path" is a costume.
- **Keep the "I".** Agentless policy prose loses the pledge. "The rule applies equally" lost to "I don't apply a quieter rule for probabilistic character generators". Narrative too: *A month later a file landed in that repo that bans the opener* hides who did it behind a date. *So I banned it.* Not *A month later I banned it*: the date was never the point.
- **"Just" is banned when it stacks the deck.** *Why not just let them make the change themselves?* shrinks the cost of what it asks, and *are you just phoning it in?* loads the question. An order's own terseness (*no thanks, no great catch, just the fix*) is a command to a machine, with no cost to shrink and no reader to lean on, and it stays.
- **No jargon without definition.** Big words are fine when they're accessible; the ban is on words that wall a reader out, not on vocabulary. Define a term once, at first use, and only when it walls the reader out. A gloss on a term the audience already holds, or one the piece links to, is padding: *a skill (a page of markdown an agent reads before it acts)* lost its parenthetical.
- **No announcements.** A sentence that grades the next point or trails an anecdote is a teaser: *That leaves a smaller problem, and it is the one that bothers people in practice.* *I learned that from a reply that was good in every way except one.* Cut it and make the point. A joint only turns the corner and promises nothing about what follows: *Which raises the question:*, *Let's talk about code review!* The joint stays; the sentence that stands in for the point goes. Throat-clearing's mid-essay cousin.
- **No dramatic turnarounds.** *I agree with all of it, and I have a stake:* and *You're right, and it's worse than I thought* stage a swing. Concede in one sentence and start the next on the point. Inflating the setup so the turn lands harder (*From a colleague I would think: this person takes review seriously. Then I reached the first two words.*) is the same move slowed down. The shape is praise, then the problem, with nothing staged between: *It named the failures and had pushed the fix before it posted. But, all I could think about was how silly the opening line felt.* No *That is a good review.* No *Then it opened with…* The allowed reversal argues; this one performs.
- **Say the feeling once.** *All I could think about was how silly the opening felt* has done the work. *I found that frustrating* after it names what the line already showed, and a later paragraph that restates the complaint (*spending a few extra tokens on "good job, boss" rubs me the wrong way*) spends it twice.
- **Dictation is notes, not copy.** When the author talks through a reaction in review, take the point and write the line. The spoken phrasing (*rubs me the wrong way*, *good job, boss*, *I found that very frustrating*) pasted into the draft came back out in the next pass. The rule is about transcribed reaction, not spoken joints: *I mean, nothing is wrong with the phrase* is the writer's own connective and stays.

## Where the breath comes from

Delight here is specific and a little self-aware. It is never punchy.

- Comic understatement: *miss, and the whole disaster is a cup on the floor*
- A human detail: *nights and weekends and too much coffee*
- A mock-exclamation that concedes the temptation, or turns a corner: *But it is so nice to have a ghostwriter! Such convenience!*, *Let's talk about code review!*
- Rueful specificity: *a script that scratches an itch and never runs again*
- A joke at your own expense, before anyone else makes it: *I'm sure about this because I've
  been the villain of it.*
- A joke that lands the argument rather than decorating it: *Slop, ironically, is sloppy.*
- A verb borrowed from the section's governing image, once: *conjured my own* under a heading about spells, *sprout up* in a section named for a garden. A second one in the same section tips into floral.

## Lint

Fail the draft if any is true. Text the piece quotes is evidence, not the draft's prose, and is exempt.

- A paragraph of jabs with no breath in it.
- Five consecutive sentences of similar length **or similar pressure**.
- Any sentence that flatters the writer.
- Any claim about what the reader will feel, conclude, or trust.
- An em-dash. A "just" that shrinks a cost or loads a question.
- A triplet whose third item adds nothing the first two didn't.
- A hedge that protects the writer rather than the fact.
- A reversal used for cleverness rather than argument.
- A sentence that grades the next point or trails a story instead of making it.
- A sentence that concedes and pivots in one breath.
- A verdict paragraph that saves its judgment for its last sentence.
- A sentence that names a feeling the previous sentence already showed.
- A clause tacked on after the landing line.
- A gloss on a term the piece already uses or links.
